## When Does a Tooth Need Emergency Extraction?

A dentist may recommend removing a tooth when other treatments cannot resolve the problem safely or predictably. Several conditions can lead to an urgent extraction.

**Severe tooth decay:** When decay destroys a large portion of the tooth, a filling or crown may no longer provide adequate protection. If the damage extends too far beneath the gumline, removal may be the most suitable option.

**Advanced dental infection:** A bacterial infection can develop inside a tooth and spread into nearby tissues. Depending on the severity, treatment may involve draining the infection, root canal therapy or extraction.

**Dental trauma:** Accidents, falls and sports injuries can crack or fracture teeth. If the damage extends below the gumline or compromises the tooth beyond repair, extraction may be necessary.

**Severe gum disease:** Advanced periodontal disease can damage the structures supporting a tooth. If the tooth becomes extremely loose and cannot be stabilised, removal may be recommended.

A dental examination helps determine which treatment is appropriate. Removing a tooth without first assessing whether it can be saved may lead to unnecessary tooth loss.

## Which Symptoms Mean You Need Urgent Dental Care?

Not every toothache requires an extraction, but certain symptoms should never be ignored. Persistent throbbing pain, pain when biting, a broken tooth, facial swelling or pus around the gums can indicate a problem that needs prompt assessment.

An unpleasant taste in the mouth, fever or swelling around the jaw may also point to an infection. These symptoms are particularly concerning when they appear together or become progressively worse.

## What Happens During an Emergency Tooth Extraction?

The procedure begins with an examination of the affected tooth and surrounding gums. Your dentist may take dental X-rays to assess the tooth roots, surrounding bone and extent of the damage.

If extraction is necessary, local anaesthesia is generally used to numb the area. You may feel pressure during the procedure, but you should not feel sharp pain. The dentist then loosens and removes the tooth using appropriate instruments. More complicated cases may require a surgical approach.

After removal, gauze is placed over the extraction site to help control bleeding and encourage a blood clot to form. Your dentist will explain how to protect the area, manage discomfort and recognise signs of complications.

The length of the appointment depends on the tooth's position, the complexity of the extraction and whether additional treatment is needed. Following the dentist's instructions is an important part of recovery.

## How Can You Manage Pain Before Your Appointment?

If you are waiting for dental care, a few sensible steps may help reduce discomfort.

- Rinse gently with warm salt water if you can do so comfortably.
- Apply a cold compress to the outside of the cheek to help reduce swelling.
- Choose soft foods and avoid chewing on the painful side.
- Use over-the-counter pain relief only as directed on the label and if it is safe for you.
- Avoid placing aspirin directly against the tooth or gums, as it can damage the tissue.

Do not attempt to remove a tooth yourself or use sharp objects to investigate the affected area. Home remedies cannot repair a fractured tooth or eliminate the source of a serious infection.

If symptoms worsen while you wait, seek further medical or dental advice. Antibiotics alone may not resolve an infected tooth when a procedure is needed to treat the underlying cause.

## What Should You Expect After a Tooth Is Removed?

Some tenderness, mild bleeding and swelling are common after an extraction. Symptoms often peak during the first two or three days and then gradually improve, although recovery varies according to the procedure and the individual.

During the initial recovery period, bite gently on the gauze as instructed, avoid vigorous rinsing and spitting, and do not use a straw if your dentist advises against it. These precautions help protect the blood clot that forms in the socket.

Choose soft foods such as yoghurt, mashed potatoes and scrambled eggs while chewing remains uncomfortable. Avoid smoking, strenuous activity during the initial recovery period and anything your dentist identifies as a risk to healing.

Contact your dentist if bleeding does not settle, pain becomes increasingly severe after initially improving, or you develop worsening swelling, fever or other signs of infection. Your dentist can assess whether further treatment is required.
